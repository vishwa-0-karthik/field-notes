TF-IDF is a family of term-weighting schemes for ranked retrieval. Term frequency (TF) rewards a term that appears in a document; inverse document frequency (IDF) gives more weight to terms found in fewer documents. Together they offer a lexical baseline that can distinguish a document about `distributed locks` from one that mentions `locks` once in a long list. The exact formula is a design choice, so record it rather than saying only “we use TF-IDF.”

## Compute a simple score

Let `N` be the number of indexed documents and `df(t)` the number containing term `t`. One common illustrative IDF is `log(N / df(t))`. A term in every document has near-zero distinguishing power; a rare term has more. Raw TF is often transformed, for example `1 + log(tf)` for positive counts, so repeating a word 100 times does not yield 100 times the score of a single occurrence. A document weight can be `tf_weight(t,d) × idf(t)`. Add weights for query terms to score a candidate.

Consider a corpus where `system` occurs in 900 of 1,000 documents and `quorum` occurs in 20. A document with `quorum` is more distinctive for a query containing both. Yet the formula alone is not relevance: a document can repeat the rare term in a misleading context, and a short title match may be more useful than several body mentions. Field boosts, phrase proximity, and document quality can supplement the lexical weight.

## Normalize for document length

Long documents naturally contain more terms and can accumulate larger raw scores. A vector-space approach treats each document as a weighted term vector and divides its dot product with the query vector by vector lengths, yielding cosine similarity. This compares direction rather than total word mass. A different normalization may be better for short product titles or for documents with large fields. Avoid assuming one analyzer and length rule suits titles, descriptions, and support articles equally.

TF-IDF needs corpus statistics. If the corpus is sharded, each shard may have slightly different `N` and `df`, which can affect cross-shard score comparability. A fast-changing catalog also changes IDF as documents arrive and disappear. In practice, small statistical drift may be acceptable, but test result stability before and after major reindexing. Explain why a score changed with the actual term contributions, not just a single opaque number.

## Use it as a baseline, then evaluate

Build a small labeled query set and compare TF-IDF variants on top-K relevance. Inspect rare terms, very common terms, long documents, and repeated keyword stuffing. Measure indexing cost because frequencies and norms require stored statistics. For exact identifiers such as SKU or error code, use a dedicated exact-match signal; TF-IDF's rarity may help, but an exact identifier deserves explicit handling.

TF-IDF does not understand synonyms or semantic similarity unless query expansion supplies related terms. It also does not enforce access permissions or freshness. Keep those as separate filters or ranking features. Modern engines often use BM25 as a stronger default lexical scorer because it explicitly saturates TF and normalizes document length, but TF-IDF remains valuable for understanding why lexical ranking works and for a transparent baseline.

## Design review

Which TF and IDF formulas are used? How are fields and document length normalized? Are statistics global or shard-local? Which query classes does this score fail, and how will a change be tested on labeled results?
