Fanout sends one change to many recipients. The recipients may be independent services consuming a business event or users receiving a social post in their feeds. Both cases trade work at publish time for cheaper or faster reads, but they have different delivery contracts. Start by naming what must reach every recipient, how quickly, and whether a late recipient can recover.

## Fan out an event to services

A publisher can send an event to a topic and let each subscriber have its own queue. Search indexing, email, and analytics then process independently. A failure in the email worker does not make the indexer lose its copy. Amazon SNS with SQS queues is one documented version of this pattern. Give each subscriber its own retry and dead-letter policy, and publish a stable event ID so each can deduplicate. If subscribers need all historical changes or want to replay from a chosen position, a retained log with separate consumer-group offsets may be a better fit.

Do not confuse broker acceptance with all subscribers completing their work. Track per-subscriber delivery age and failed actions. Define what happens when a new subscriber joins: it may receive only future events, require a backfill from the source of truth, or replay a retained log. A producer should not assume that a notification consumer has caught up when it responds to the original request.

## Fan out a post to follower feeds

For a social feed, *fanout on write* inserts a post reference into each follower's inbox when the author publishes. Feed reads become cheap, but publish cost grows with follower count. A celebrity with ten million followers can create a write storm and a long propagation tail. *Fanout on read* stores the post once and merges posts from followed authors when a user opens the feed. Publishing is cheap, but reads need more candidate retrieval, merging, and ranking. A hybrid precomputes normal authors' inbox entries and fetches very large authors' posts during reads.

The inbox should store post IDs and ranking metadata rather than duplicate full post bodies. Use a stable `(viewer_id, post_id)` identity to make retries safe. Partition fanout work by viewer or recipient range, and keep a cursor or durable job record so a worker can resume midway. A user's follower relationship can change while a post is propagating: decide whether feed eligibility is based on the relationship at publish time or read time. For privacy-sensitive accounts, enforce current visibility again on read even if an old inbox entry exists.

## Bound amplification and repair partial delivery

The rough write cost is `posts_per_second × average_follower_count`, but the average hides heavy-tailed authors. Capacity planning needs high-percentile follower counts and burst rates. Use per-author or per-tenant quotas and bounded worker concurrency. Separate hot authors from ordinary fanout so one post cannot consume all queue capacity. Batch database writes where safe, but ensure each batch can be retried without creating duplicates.

Measure time from publish to visible feed entry at high percentiles, not only queue depth. Keep a reconciliation path that compares a post's intended recipients with completed batches and repairs gaps. Feed ranking can tolerate some delay; private or safety-critical notifications may require a stricter delivery contract. State that contract explicitly and choose write, read, or hybrid fanout accordingly.

## Design review

Who are the recipients, and what does delivery mean? Can each subscriber or follower batch retry independently? How are new subscribers and partial fanout repaired? What happens for an author with orders of magnitude more followers than average? Is visibility rechecked when a feed is read?
