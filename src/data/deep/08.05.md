A distributed task scheduler decides when a job becomes eligible, then hands it to workers that execute it. It has two related but different responsibilities: maintain the schedule reliably, and deliver each due occurrence despite process failures. A timer running in every application replica duplicates work. A single timer avoids duplicates while it runs, but becomes a point of failure unless another scheduler can take over.

## Represent a schedule as data

Store a schedule ID, task type, arguments reference, time zone, recurrence rule, next due time, enabled state, and version. Give each occurrence a stable identity such as `(schedule_id, scheduled_for)`. The identity lets a worker reject duplicates when a scheduler retries a publish or leadership changes mid-tick. Record both the intended fire time and actual enqueue time so missed deadlines can be measured.

Choose explicit semantics for missed runs. After an outage, should a daily report run once for each missed day, only for the latest day, or not at all? There is no universal answer: invoice settlement may require every interval, while a cache refresh may only need the newest. Limit catch-up bursts so a recovered scheduler does not flood workers. Clarify daylight-saving transitions for schedules expressed in a local time zone; store instants in UTC and keep the original time zone for recurrence calculation.

## Coordinate schedulers and workers

One implementation uses a database row lock or leased leadership so one scheduler scans due rows. It advances `next_due_at` and writes occurrence rows transactionally, then a relay enqueues them. Another partitions schedules by ID and leases shards across schedulers. The lease protects who may schedule, but failover can still produce duplicate occurrences near the boundary. Enforce a unique constraint on the occurrence identity and make task effects idempotent. Celery's periodic-task documentation likewise warns that multiple schedulers for the same schedule can cause duplicate tasks.

Workers should claim occurrences with a lease, heartbeat long-running work, and acknowledge after a durable result. If a worker dies after performing the side effect but before acknowledgment, the task may run again. A payment task needs an idempotency key at the payment provider or a unique business transition, not merely a scheduler lock. Use retries with exponential backoff and a terminal failure state that operators can inspect and requeue.

## Control load and observe deadlines

Scheduling a million notifications at exactly 09:00 creates a burst even if the scheduler itself is fast. Spread eligible jobs over a defined window when the product permits it, use separate queues for priority classes, and cap worker concurrency to downstream capacity. Store payload references instead of large blobs in the schedule table. Index by enabled state and next due time; shard when the due scan or enqueue rate exceeds one database's practical capacity.

Measure due occurrences, enqueue delay, start delay, run duration, retry count, and missed or skipped occurrences. Compare `scheduled_for` with completion time for the real SLA. An empty queue does not prove that schedules fired: the scheduler might be down and have produced nothing. A periodic canary schedule with an end-to-end completion check helps reveal that failure.

## Design review

What happens to missed runs after an outage? How is a due occurrence identified across failover? Can the same effect execute twice safely? What limits a catch-up burst? Which measurement proves scheduled work actually completed by its deadline?
