An append-only stream layer stores a sequence of records so producers can write once and consumers can read at their own pace. Partitioned logs make ordering local to each partition and allow throughput to scale horizontally. The stream is storage with retention and replay semantics, not merely a transient message pipe.

## Make offsets meaningful

Each partition assigns increasing offsets to appended records. A consumer asks for records after its last processed offset and can replay from an earlier point while retention permits. The offset is a position in one partition, not a global timestamp or order across all partitions. If events for one entity must be processed in order, use a stable partition key that sends them to the same partition. A large or hot entity can then become that partition's bottleneck.

Files can be organized into append-only segments with an offset index. Appends are sequential, and old segments can be deleted after a retention period. Kafka's log implementation documents segment recovery and validation of the newest segment after restart. The service must define what happens to a partially appended batch and when an acknowledgement means replicas have enough copies.

## Separate retention from consumption

A consumer committing offset 500 does not delete records 0–500 for every other consumer. Retention is a stream policy based on time or size, while each consumer tracks its own position. A slow consumer can fall behind the earliest retained offset and must rebuild from a snapshot or another source. Monitor time and bytes between consumer position and log end, not just whether the consumer process is alive.

Compacted streams retain recent values by key rather than every historical event. This is useful for rebuilding current state, but it is not a complete audit trail. Tombstones may remove keys after a retention window, so consumers that have been offline too long can miss a deletion if they rely only on an old snapshot. Match log cleanup policy to recovery and compliance needs.

## Decide the delivery contract

A producer can time out after the broker appended a record but before it received the acknowledgement. Retrying can duplicate the record unless producer IDs, sequence numbers, or application idempotency address that ambiguity. Consumers may process a record and crash before committing their offset; on restart they see it again. Make side effects idempotent or atomically commit the effect and offset within one authority when possible.

Replication protects against broker failure, but each stream has a chosen acknowledgement and leader-failover rule. A follower that is behind may not have the latest acknowledged records under a weak configuration. Test failover, segment corruption, and retention during consumer outages. Track append latency, replication lag, under-replicated partitions, segment count, disk use, and replay throughput.

Give operators a documented way to pause a producer or consumer when these measures cross safe limits. Adding partitions later can improve throughput, but it may change which partition receives a key and therefore alter its ordering history.

## Design review

What order is promised within and across partitions? Can consumers replay far enough back to recover? When is an append acknowledged? What happens after a producer timeout or a consumer crash between processing and offset commit?
