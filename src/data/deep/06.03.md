Replication keeps copies of data on more than one node. It can improve survival after a node failure, place reads nearer users, and sometimes spread read traffic. It also creates the question of when a write is safe to acknowledge and what a reader may see on another replica. State those semantics before drawing arrows between servers.

## Choose the write acknowledgement point

With asynchronous replication, a leader can acknowledge after a local commit and send the change later. This keeps write latency low, but a leader failure before a follower receives the change may lose an acknowledged write. Synchronous replication waits for other nodes before acknowledgement, improving durability against a single-node loss at the cost of latency and potentially availability. A quorum design commits when enough replicas confirm, assuming the quorum and failure model are correctly configured.

“Replicated” is not the same as “durable.” A follower may have received a write only in memory. A region-wide incident can remove copies that were placed in the same failure domain. Define whether acknowledgement means written to a durable log, applied to an index, or visible for reads. For every topology, test the time window between these milestones.

## Understand leader and multi-leader patterns

A single leader orders writes for its shard and simplifies conflict handling. Followers can answer stale reads unless the client waits for a known applied position or reads from the leader. Leader failover must avoid two active leaders accepting conflicting writes. Consensus-backed logs such as Raft use terms and majority agreement to choose a leader and commit an ordered sequence, but availability still requires a reachable majority.

Multi-leader or leaderless systems can accept writes in more places. Dynamo's design emphasizes high availability for a key-value workload and includes mechanisms for divergent versions and later reconciliation. That choice makes conflict semantics visible to the data model. Last-write-wins is simple but can discard an update when clocks differ or concurrent users write. A shopping cart can merge sets or preserve siblings; a bank ledger needs stronger ordering. The conflict policy is a product rule.

## Plan for lag, repair, and reads

Replication is a continuous pipeline. Monitor the distance between primary commit and replica apply, not just whether connections are up. A replica can be healthy but minutes behind after a large import or slow disk. Reads from it may violate read-your-writes. During catch-up, keep source logs long enough that a lagging replica can recover; otherwise it may need a full snapshot. Background repair and anti-entropy can fix missed updates in eventually consistent systems, but they consume bandwidth and need a way to identify divergent versions.

Place replicas across failure domains according to the incident you need to survive. Replication does not replace backup: an accidental delete or corrupted update can replicate faithfully to every node. Keep point-in-time recovery or immutable backups with tested restore procedures. A read replica also does not automatically help when the leader's write path is the bottleneck.

## Design review

When is a write acknowledged? Which replica serves a read after the client just wrote? How is a new leader chosen, and can acknowledged data be lost? What repairs divergence? Which failures do backups cover that replicas do not?
