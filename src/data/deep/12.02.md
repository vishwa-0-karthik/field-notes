Geohash encodes a latitude/longitude point as a string whose prefixes represent progressively smaller rectangular cells. The prefix property makes it convenient to store in a key-value or ordered index: objects sharing a prefix are geographically close at that resolution. But nearby points can land on opposite sides of a cell boundary, and rectangle dimensions vary by latitude. A prefix query is a candidate search, not a complete distance query.

## Use prefixes to narrow the search

The encoding repeatedly bisects longitude and latitude ranges, interleaves the decisions, and maps bits to characters. A short prefix covers a large rectangle; each additional character selects one of its child rectangles. Choose precision from the desired cell size and typical density. A city directory might use a coarser prefix than a doorstep delivery feature. Longer prefixes reduce candidates but increase the number of neighboring cells that must be searched around boundaries.

For a radius query, compute the query point's cell and the neighboring cells that intersect the radius. Fetch candidates from all relevant prefixes, then compute exact geodesic distance and filter. Searching only the query's prefix misses a café one meter away across a cell edge. Near the poles and antimeridian, coordinate wrapping makes neighbors especially important. Cell width shrinks with latitude while height is more stable, so “precision 7 means 150 meters” is not a universal guarantee.

## Store and route consistently

Store both the original coordinates and geohash. The coordinates support exact calculations and map display; the hash supports prefix lookup and coarse routing. Keep the hash precision/version with the index configuration. If a record's location changes, update its old and new prefix entries atomically or make the index rebuildable, otherwise it may appear in both places or neither. Partitioning by a short prefix can localize regional queries, but a dense downtown cell may become a hot partition.

For global nearest-neighbor requests, expand outward through cells until the candidates include enough results and a distance lower bound proves no unvisited cell can contain a closer point. A fixed “search 3x3 neighbors” is only correct for bounded query radii and chosen precision. For varying radius, enumerate all intersecting cells or use a spatial database index. After retrieval, exact distance, visibility, and availability filters still determine the answer.

A useful operational detail is to keep the query's chosen precision separate from its maximum radius. A larger search radius may require progressively coarser or more numerous cells; silently truncating the cell list creates geographic blind spots. Emit the cells searched and the exact-filter result count in traces so a miss can be reproduced.

## Measure the approximation boundary

Benchmark candidate-to-result ratio, number of cells queried, hot-prefix skew, and missed-neighbor rate against exact spatial queries. Test points at cell edges, across 180° longitude, near the poles, and in both dense and sparse regions. A geohash prefix is good for sharding, bounding boxes, and coarse scans; it is poor as a direct proof of exact distance or uniformly sized area. Use it where its simple string ordering fits the storage layer, then preserve the geometry semantics in the final filter.

## Design review

Which precision meets the density and radius needs? How are adjacent cells selected completely? How are changing locations removed from old prefixes? Can one dense prefix become hot? What exact distance calculation rejects false positives?
