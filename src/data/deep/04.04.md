A graph database makes relationships first-class data. It can be valuable when the primary question is a traversal: follow several kinds of edges from one entity, filter along a path, or ask how two entities are connected. It is less compelling when the workload is mostly point lookups, flat aggregates, or simple joins that a relational database already handles well.

## Ask a path-shaped question

Consider a fraud investigation system. Accounts use devices, devices use IP addresses, and payment cards are linked to accounts. An investigator asks: “Which other accounts share a device with this account, and did any of them use the same card within two hops?” Nodes represent accounts, devices, IPs, and cards; typed relationships represent `USES_DEVICE`, `USES_IP`, and `PAID_WITH`. A traversal follows only relevant edges rather than repeatedly joining several large tables to discover an unknown path.

Neo4j's property graph model has nodes, typed directed relationships, and properties on both. That makes relationship semantics visible in the query. But a graph model does not automatically make every graph query cheap. A high-degree node, such as a shared public IP, can fan out to millions of neighbors. Bound traversal depth, filter edge types and times, and inspect query plans.

| Question | Graph fit | Alternative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One account by ID | Little special benefit | Indexed relational or key-value lookup |
| Friends of friends with filters | Often strong | Repeated joins or precomputed view |
| Total sales by region | Usually not its main strength | Relational or columnar aggregation |
| Shortest relationship path | Natural graph operation | Custom traversal over adjacency tables |

## Model edges carefully

An edge should represent a real domain relationship with direction and lifecycle. `ACCOUNT_USES_DEVICE` may need first-seen and last-seen timestamps; a device observed once years ago should not have the same meaning as an active session. Decide whether parallel edges are allowed, whether one edge is updated, and how deletion propagates. Avoid creating a generic `RELATED_TO` edge for every relationship; it makes traversals harder to filter and reason about.

Constraints still matter. Unique account IDs and device fingerprints need indexes. Authorization and privacy matter when traversals can reveal indirect connections between people. A query that begins from an authorized account may reach other accounts the caller should not see. Filter results and possibly intermediate paths according to policy.

## Count the integration cost

A graph database often starts as a derived model from transactional records. If the order system remains authoritative, event pipelines must add and remove edges when records change. Define lag, replay, and reconciliation. A stale fraud graph may miss a recent link; a stale social graph may show an unfollowed connection. If graph writes are authoritative, the team needs backup, restore, availability, and transaction behavior appropriate to that role.

Before adding a new store, try a representative relational query with indexes or a materialized adjacency table. If common queries involve variable-depth traversals and repeated relationship filtering, a graph engine may justify itself. Benchmark the actual graph shape, especially high-degree nodes, not only a neat demonstration dataset.

## Design review

What is the central multi-hop query, and how deep can it go? Which edges are high-degree? Is the graph authoritative or derived? How are edge updates, deletions, and authorization reflected in traversals?
