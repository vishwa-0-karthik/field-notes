Back-of-the-envelope planning turns product scale into rough orders of magnitude. Its purpose is to reveal bottlenecks and unreasonable assumptions before detailed benchmarking, not to predict exact cloud bills. Keep units visible and show the arithmetic so another engineer can change the assumptions.

## Begin with workload shape

Suppose a note-sharing product has one million daily active users. Each creates 0.2 notes per day and reads 30 notes per day. That is about 200,000 writes/day and 30 million reads/day. Divide by 86,400 seconds: roughly 2.3 average writes/s and 347 average reads/s. If a busy hour is five times the daily average, plan initially for about 12 writes/s and 1,740 reads/s. The multiplier is an assumption that must later be checked against traffic data.

Requests are not all equal. A list request may fetch twenty note summaries; a full-text search may scan an index; an upload may move megabytes. Break work down by endpoint and by read/write mix. A single “QPS” number can conceal a database-heavy query or a bandwidth-heavy media endpoint.

## Estimate storage and transfer separately

If an average note body is 4 KB and metadata adds 1 KB, raw new data is about 200,000 × 5 KB = 1 GB/day, or 365 GB/year before indexes, replicas, logs, versions, backups, and deletion. A rough 3× physical multiplier would make the first-year planning figure about 1.1 TB. The multiplier is not a database law; it is a placeholder until schema and retention policy are known.

For reads, suppose each response transfers 8 KB. Thirty million reads/day transfer about 240 GB/day, or about 2.8 MB/s on average. At a five-times busy-hour factor, the rough edge rate is 14 MB/s. Add protocol overhead, images, and CDN effects separately. Do not confuse network egress with stored data volume.

| Quantity | Rough formula | Example result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Average read rate | reads/day ÷ 86,400 | 347/s |
| Peak read rate | average × peak factor | 1,740/s |
| Annual raw storage | writes/day × bytes/write × 365 | 365 GB |
| Daily response bytes | reads/day × bytes/response | 240 GB |

## Convert rate into concurrency and capacity

Little's Law gives a useful approximation: average in-flight requests ≈ arrival rate × average time in the system. At 1,740 requests/s and 100 ms average latency, roughly 174 requests are in flight. If a dependency slows to one second, the same arrival rate implies roughly 1,740 in-flight requests. This is why latency spikes can exhaust connection pools and memory even when incoming QPS has not risen.

Estimate sustainable capacity per instance through load testing. If one instance safely handles 300 requests/s at the target p99 latency, 1,740 requests/s needs about six instances for raw throughput. Add headroom and failure capacity: if one instance can fail without breaching the target, seven or more may be needed. CPU, database connections, queue depth, and p99 latency may set a lower safe rate than raw throughput.

## Stress the assumptions

Run sensitivity checks: what if peak factor is 15 rather than five, note size doubles, or a new feature multiplies reads? A design that changes completely under a small assumption shift needs a clearer scaling path. Record which numbers came from measurement, which from product forecasts, and which were invented for the exercise. AWS reliability guidance recommends scaling metrics that correlate with demand and enough headroom while new capacity starts; that is more actionable than a fixed fleet chosen from average traffic.

## Design review

Can you recompute the estimate from the written inputs? Have you separated request rate, concurrency, storage, and transfer? What breaks first when latency rises or one instance disappears? Which measurement would replace the weakest assumption?
