# SLOs and error budgets

A service level objective (SLO) is a measurable target for a user-relevant aspect of service quality over a stated window. It turns vague goals like “fast and reliable” into a decision tool for engineering and product teams. An SLO is built from a service level indicator (SLI), such as the fraction of eligible requests that succeed or finish under a latency threshold, and a target such as 99.9% over 28 days. The error budget is the allowed unreliability: one minus the objective.

## Choose indicators users can feel

Define the request population and exclusions precisely. If health checks or internal retries count differently, document the reason. A successful HTTP status may still represent failure if the response is unusable; a cache hit could still be stale; a background job may be “available” while delayed beyond its deadline. For latency, decide whether the SLI is a threshold fraction, a percentile, or a user-journey duration. For batch work, completion within an agreed age may be more meaningful than per-request latency. Measure at the boundary that approximates what the user observes and validate it against client-side evidence.

Use event-based SLIs when requests are independently countable, or time-based SLIs when the service is judged by intervals of healthy operation. Event-based ratios avoid a quiet period looking healthy just because there were few measurements. Split objectives only when different user journeys genuinely have different expectations. Too many SLOs create policy noise; too few hide which audience is harmed. A service may have an availability SLO and a latency SLO, but each needs an accountable owner, data source, and alert path.

## Spend budget intentionally

If a 99.9% objective is measured over a month, the remaining 0.1% is the budget for bad events in that window. The budget is not a target to consume; it is a way to balance release speed and reliability investment. A team can set policy that normal changes proceed while budget remains, but repeated or fast budget burn pauses risky changes and directs capacity toward mitigation. The response should be based on user impact and cause, not punishment. A dependency outage can spend the service's budget even when application code is healthy, which gives a reason to reduce dependency risk or adjust the dependency contract.

Use both a long rolling window and a short burn-rate alert. A slow degradation may consume budget over weeks; a sharp outage may exhaust it in hours. Multi-window burn alerts catch urgent incidents while reducing noise from brief blips. Budget accounting should be transparent and robust to telemetry gaps. If the SLI pipeline fails, mark budget data as unknown rather than treating missing events as successful requests. Avoid changing an objective retroactively to erase a miss; revise it forward with documented reasoning and stakeholder agreement.

Review objectives against actual user expectations and product economics. A database-backed internal tool may not need the same availability as checkout. Conversely, a high SLO that costs heavily to achieve without changing user outcomes may be wasteful. Use error budget reports to prioritize investments such as graceful degradation, dependency isolation, rollout controls, and repair automation. Track objective attainment, budget burn, and outcome of reliability work, not just uptime claims.

## Design review

Is the SLI user-centered and precisely defined? Does the denominator represent real eligible traffic? Are short and long burn windows actionable? What policy follows when the budget is nearly spent? How are telemetry gaps and external dependency failures represented?
