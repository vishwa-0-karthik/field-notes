Stop words are very common terms that some search pipelines remove or downweight. Champion lists are precomputed short lists of strong documents for a term. Both can reduce query work, but both can discard information. The right question is whether their latency and storage savings are worth the retrieval errors for the product's actual queries.

## Treat stop words as a product choice

In a general English corpus, words such as `the` may occur in so many documents that their postings are expensive and their ranking value is small. Dropping them from an index saves space. But a query such as `to be or not to be`, a product named `The Ordinary`, or a legal phrase can depend on those exact words and their positions. Stanford's retrieval text explains that modern systems often keep stop words because compression and query optimization make them manageable, while phrase queries benefit from retaining them.

Use field-specific behavior. A title or exact-name field may preserve all tokens, while a broad body field may use a different analyzer. If stop words are removed at index time, the query analyzer must make compatible choices; otherwise a user phrase can become impossible to match correctly. Keep the original text for highlighting and display. Before changing an analyzer, estimate reindex cost and run tests for quoted phrases, names, and short queries.

## Limit scoring with champion lists

For each term, a champion list holds the `r` documents with the highest precomputed term weights. A query can union the champion lists of its terms, score only those candidates, and answer quickly. This is an approximation. A document that is moderately strong for two terms may rank above documents that are champions for only one term, yet never enter the candidate set. If fewer than K credible results are found, fall back to broader postings or another index tier.

Choose `r` based on corpus size, query mix, and required recall. One value for all terms may be wasteful: rare terms may need a different policy from extremely common ones. Rebuild or update champions as documents and weights change; stale lists can make fresh documents hard to find even after they are indexed. A business rule that excludes out-of-stock or private documents can reduce the usable candidates further, so filtering must be included in recall tests.

## Observe the quality budget

Compare the fast path's top-K with a full-search baseline on representative queries. Measure overlap, recall of judged relevant documents, fallback frequency, p95 latency, and postings read. Examine the worst queries, not only average overlap. A system that always uses the full index when it misses its fast target may have poor tail latency, so record the conditions that trigger fallback and set a deadline policy.

Stop-word removal and champion pruning affect different stages. One changes which terms are represented; the other changes which documents receive full scoring. Combining them can multiply misses, especially for short phrases. Start with a complete, correct baseline and add pruning only when profiling shows a real cost. The retrieval contract should state whether exact phrase and identifier queries bypass approximate pruning.

## Design review

Which queries require common words? Are index and query analyzers compatible? How are champion lists refreshed? What recall is lost after filters? Which query types bypass pruning, and what is the fallback when the first tier has too few results?
