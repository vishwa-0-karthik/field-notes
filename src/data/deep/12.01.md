Nearby search finds objects within a distance of a point and returns the closest useful results. Examples include restaurants around a user, couriers near a pickup, and devices within a service radius. The design must get coordinate order, distance units, and Earth geometry right before optimizing the index. Latitude and longitude are not ordinary Cartesian meters.

## Filter candidates, then rank by distance

Store each point with an explicit coordinate reference system and index. A geospatial index narrows candidates by bounding boxes or cells; the final predicate computes actual distance. PostGIS recommends `ST_DWithin` for radius filtering because it can use a spatial index, while `ST_Distance` alone may scan every row. With `geography`, `ST_DWithin` takes meters and accounts for Earth's shape; with projected `geometry`, distance units come from the spatial reference. PostGIS also documents that point X is longitude and Y is latitude, a common source of swapped-coordinate errors.

A typical query first filters by service eligibility and an indexed radius, then orders a bounded result set by exact distance and possibly rating, availability, or ETA. “Nearest” and “best” are different ranks. A straight-line radius can be a candidate filter for a driving-distance result, but routes, bridges, and road closures require a routing service. Return distance to the client with its units and calculation basis.

## Handle boundaries and geography

Near the poles or across the antimeridian, a rectangular longitude/latitude box can wrap or distort. Use geography-aware operations or split the search region into safe boxes before applying the exact distance test. A radius query may cover several spatial index cells; cell indexing is a candidate generator and can include false positives. Exact filtering is required at the end. If locations are noisy, GPS accuracy should influence radius and ranking rather than pretending every point is exact.

Freshness is often more important than raw query speed. A driver's location may expire after a short TTL, while a restaurant coordinate is mostly static. Store observation time and accuracy, discard stale moving objects, and update their location independently from profile data. A nearby result from a courier last seen an hour ago is misleading even if the distance calculation is perfect.

## Scale read and update paths

For a moderate catalog, a database spatial index is straightforward and supports exact predicates. For high-rate moving objects, keep a hot location index partitioned by city, cell, or service region, with durable records elsewhere. Geospatial cells can route candidate queries to relevant partitions, but neighboring cell coverage must be complete around the radius. Cache popular center-and-radius queries only for stable datasets and include time/freshness in invalidation.

Measure candidate count, exact-distance computations, p95 latency, location age, coordinate validation failures, and result coverage at cell or city borders. Test longitude wraparound, zero radius, invalid coordinates, and sparse areas. The query should state what qualifies as “near,” how stale locations are treated, and what tie-breaker decides equal-distance results.

## Design review

What distance metric and units does the product promise? Which indexed filter narrows candidates? Are coordinates stored with a known CRS and in lon/lat order? How does the query handle stale or noisy moving points? Are cell or box candidates exact-filtered before display?
