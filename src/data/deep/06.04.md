CAP is a statement about what a replicated service can guarantee during a network partition. PACELC adds the tradeoff that remains during normal operation: lower latency often competes with stronger consistency across distant replicas. Neither is a three-letter score for an entire product. Use them to ask what each operation does when nodes cannot communicate and when they can.

## Read the CAP terms precisely

In the Gilbert and Lynch formulation, consistency means a linearizable shared object: completed operations appear in one real-time-respecting order. Availability means every request to a nonfailed node eventually gets a response. A partition means messages between parts of the system can be lost or delayed indefinitely. During that partition, a service cannot guarantee both linearizable behavior and responses from both sides for operations on the same data.

Imagine two replicas separated by a broken link. A client writes `x = 2` to one side and receives success. A client on the other side asks for `x`. If it answers immediately with old value `1`, linearizability is broken. If it waits until communication returns, availability as defined by CAP is broken. The service can choose to reject or stall some requests to preserve consistency, or answer with weaker consistency. It can also choose differently for different operations and keys.

“Partition tolerance” is not a feature that can be switched off in a real network. A system can assume partitions are outside its operating envelope, but then it must say what happens when that assumption fails. Also, CAP availability is a formal liveness property, not a percentage such as 99.9% uptime. Operational availability includes latency limits, which the theorem's definition does not fully capture.

## Add the normal-case choice with PACELC

Daniel Abadi's PACELC framing says: if there is a partition (P), choose how to trade availability (A) against consistency (C); else (E), choose how to trade latency (L) against consistency (C). Strongly coordinated writes across distant regions may need extra message rounds or waiting before acknowledgement. A local write followed by asynchronous replication can respond sooner, but a reader elsewhere may see an old value even with a healthy network.

This explains why a system might offer an eventually consistent read mode in normal operation: it is seeking lower latency, not reacting to an active partition. A design can also have several points on the spectrum. One API may read from a local replica; another may wait for a quorum or route to a leader. The selected mode should follow the business invariant and latency budget.

## Apply the frameworks to one operation

Take “reserve the last available seat.” During a partition, allowing both sides to confirm the reservation risks overselling. A majority-based authority can reject the minority side until connectivity returns. For “show recommended articles,” a temporarily old list may be acceptable, so the local replica can keep serving. During normal operation, seat reservation may tolerate a cross-region round trip, while recommendations may prioritize local response time.

Do not assign a database a permanent “CP” or “AP” badge without checking its configuration, read mode, write acknowledgement, and client routing. Caches and queues can weaken the application contract even if the underlying database offers strong operations. Test the actual request path under message loss, delay, and replica lag.

## Design review

Which exact operation requires a real-time-respecting order? What does each partition side return when it cannot contact the other? What latency does coordination add in the healthy case? Are the chosen behaviors visible in the API contract?
