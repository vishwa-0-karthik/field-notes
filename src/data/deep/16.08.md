# Design: nearby search

Nearby search returns entities within a distance of a user or map region, often ordered by distance, relevance, or availability. Examples include restaurants, couriers, charging stations, and service professionals. The system needs a clear distance model, fast candidate generation, current location data, and a final exact predicate. Coordinate systems, freshness, and filtering semantics matter more than the choice of index alone.

## Candidate retrieval and ranking

For a modest static catalog, a relational database with a spatial index can filter candidates by radius and calculate exact distance. A geospatial cell system such as geohash, S2, or H3 can partition very large datasets and route a query to intersecting cells. Cell covers may include false positives and a radius can span neighboring cells, so fetch all relevant cells and apply an exact distance calculation before returning results. Near cell boundaries or the antimeridian, ensure the cover includes every candidate. Latitude and longitude order and units must be explicit.

Filter by eligibility before ranking where possible: open status, service type, capacity, safety restrictions, or user-selected constraints. The nearest item may not be the best item. Combine exact distance with ranking features such as rating, ETA, availability, and personalization, while making the meaning of “nearby” understandable. Straight-line distance is a useful candidate filter but is not road-network distance. For driving or delivery, call a routing/ETA service on a bounded top candidate set rather than routing every nearby point.

## Freshness and scaling

Static business locations change infrequently and can be indexed durably. Moving devices and couriers update frequently; keep their latest coordinate and observation timestamp in a hot location store, separate from profile data. Expire stale locations and account for GPS accuracy. Partition by city, service area, or spatial cell to keep local searches local. Large urban cells may need subdivision; rural areas may be sparse. Popular map centers can be cached briefly, but include filters and data freshness in the cache contract.

Location is sensitive personal data. Collect it only when the feature needs it, enforce access by purpose, and avoid putting precise coordinates in general logs or long-retention analytics. Give clients a clear stale/unknown state instead of treating old coordinates as current. A privacy-preserving aggregate view may be appropriate for public maps.

Measure query latency, candidate count, exact-distance work, result coverage, stale-location share, cell fanout, routing calls per search, and cache hit ratio. Test points exactly on the radius boundary, coordinate swap, zero radius, antimeridian crossing, stale moving assets, and cell-border coverage. A useful design specifies whether the query optimizes radial distance, travel time, or best available match.

At large scale, query fanout can dominate even when each cell lookup is fast. Batch cell reads, cap the number of candidate cells, and avoid a separate remote call for every result attribute. If the product needs a viewport query rather than a point radius, generate a cover for the bounding polygon and refine the boundary locally. Recompute or invalidate the query when the viewer moves far enough that the previous result set no longer represents their location.

## Design review

What distance metric and units are promised? Which spatial index generates candidates, and is the final predicate exact? How are stale or inaccurate moving locations handled? Does route ETA apply to all candidates or a bounded subset? How are precise coordinates protected and retained?
