# Disaster recovery

Disaster recovery (DR) restores service after a failure that removes a substantial part of the normal production environment, such as a region outage, account compromise, or data-center loss. It differs from high availability: HA handles smaller failures automatically within the normal operating environment, while DR may require a deliberate activation, data recovery, and traffic cutover. Define recovery point objective (RPO) and recovery time objective (RTO) for each critical user journey, then choose a strategy that meets those objectives at an acceptable cost.

## Choose a recovery topology

Backup and restore keeps a separate copy of data and recreates infrastructure after failure. It is cost-efficient but often has the longest RTO. Pilot light keeps core data replication and minimal infrastructure warm, then scales services after activation. Warm standby runs a reduced but functional copy and can cut over faster at higher cost. Active-active serves traffic from multiple regions, but adds consistency, routing, and operational complexity. These are points on a cost and recovery spectrum, not labels that guarantee a specific RTO.

Replicate the state needed to serve requests, not just the main database. This may include object storage, encryption keys, identity configuration, secrets, container images, DNS records, queues, feature flags, and required external service credentials. Infrastructure as code reduces manual recreation errors, but the recovery environment must be tested with the exact production configuration and quotas. Cross-region replication can lag or replicate corruption; define which data is authoritative and how operators choose the last safe recovery point.

## Failover without a split brain

Before shifting traffic, determine whether the original region is truly unavailable or merely isolated from the control plane. If both regions can accept writes, clients may create conflicting histories. Use a clear writer-election or fencing mechanism: the old region must be prevented from writing before the recovery region becomes authoritative. DNS TTLs and client caches can delay traffic movement; health checks need to distinguish user-serving health from an endpoint that only answers TCP. After failover, preserve the old environment for forensic analysis and avoid automatic failback until data has been reconciled.

Recovery playbooks should define authority, technical steps, decision thresholds, communications, and validation queries. Run game days that exercise full cutover, including application secrets, queues, search indexes, object access, and third-party dependencies. Measure actual recovery time and data loss against the targets. Test both a planned regional evacuation and a scenario where the primary is unreachable and its final writes are unknown. A recovery that restores compute but not authorization or user-visible data is incomplete.

After the primary returns, reconciling divergent data and switching back can be riskier than failover. Treat failback as a separate planned migration: establish a new source of truth, replicate in one direction, validate parity, and move traffic gradually. Keep the DR topology simple enough that an on-call team can operate it under stress. Review objectives after major architecture or business changes, and retain evidence from exercises.

## Design review

What failure domain does the plan cover? Which state is replicated and with what lag? How is the old writer fenced before promotion? Has end-to-end RTO been measured in a real exercise? What exact reconciliation process governs failback?
