# Incident response

Incident response is a coordinated way to reduce user harm while preserving enough information to understand what happened. It is a system design problem because safe recovery depends on clear ownership, observable state, reversible controls, and dependencies that can be isolated. During an incident, the first job is to restore a safe level of service; detailed root-cause analysis can follow once the system is stable.

## Establish a simple command structure

When impact is broad or uncertain, name an incident lead who coordinates the response, an operations lead who runs technical mitigation, and a communications lead who updates stakeholders. One person can fill multiple roles in a small event, but make the responsibilities explicit. Keep a shared timeline with timestamps, observations, hypotheses, actions, and outcomes. This reduces duplicate work and makes shift handoff possible. Assign tasks by owner and expected update time; avoid several people independently changing the same production control.

Triage from user symptoms and service objectives: which journeys fail, how many users, which regions, and what changed? Compare recent deployments, configuration, dependency health, queue lag, saturation, and error rates. Use traces and logs to localize the path, but verify that the signal is current and covers the affected population. Establish an initial severity based on impact, not on how easy the technical cause seems. If evidence is incomplete, mitigate conservatively and state uncertainty.

## Prefer reversible mitigation

Choose controls that reduce impact quickly and can be rolled back: disable a feature flag, shed optional work, shift traffic, pause a rollout, scale a constrained pool, or fail over to a known-good dependency. Understand secondary effects before acting: routing all traffic away from a region can overload the remaining regions, and retry storms can amplify an outage. Use idempotent, auditable operations where possible. Make one change at a time when practical, then check the expected user-facing signal before continuing. A rollback may not reverse a database migration or external side effect, so identify those risks before triggering it.

Protect responders from fatigue and information overload. Keep one incident channel as the source of truth, publish concise updates at a predictable cadence, and rotate people for long events. Do not page every team for every alert; route alerts to the owner who can act. If a mitigation is uncertain, stop risky changes and preserve evidence. Once service is restored, verify recovery across regions, clients, queues, and dependent workflows; a green dashboard can hide a growing backlog.

## Learn and close the loop

Afterward, write a blameless review focused on contributing conditions and missing defenses. Include the user impact timeline, detection and mitigation gaps, what went well, and a small set of owned actions with dates. Actions should reduce recurrence or impact—for example, add a canary, cap retries, improve a dashboard, or make a failover safe. Track completion and verify the change; a postmortem without follow-through is an archive, not a reliability mechanism. Share relevant details with support and affected users according to the communication policy.

Practice response with game days and realistic failure drills. Include alert receipt, access to runbooks, dependency ownership, rollback, and communications. Measure time to detect, acknowledge, mitigate, and recover, but use these metrics to improve the system rather than grade individuals. The response process should remain usable when chat, dashboards, or the primary region are themselves impaired.

## Design review

Who coordinates decisions and communications? Which mitigations are reversible? Can one action overload a surviving region or amplify retries? How is recovery verified beyond the first green graph? Are follow-up actions owned and tested?
