Leader election selects a process to coordinate work for a period. The hard part is not choosing a name; it is preventing an old leader and a new leader from both making authoritative changes. Network delays can make a healthy process look dead, and a paused process can resume after its lease has expired. Tie leadership to a term, epoch, or fencing token that the protected resource can recognize.

## See why a heartbeat is insufficient

Suppose worker A owns a scheduled export and stops sending heartbeats during a long pause. Worker B takes over. A resumes and continues writing output. If both can publish the final file, users may see duplicate or inconsistent results. A heartbeat only tells observers that A was recently reachable. It does not revoke A's ability to act after observers choose B.

Raft addresses leadership for its replicated log using terms and majority elections. A candidate wins one term by collecting votes from a majority, and a node that sees a higher term steps down. Log freshness restricts which candidate can lead, so a newly elected leader carries committed history. The protected log checks terms when accepting entries. This is stronger than a shared database row that merely says `leader_id = A` without fencing subsequent operations.

## Choose an authority for the lease

An application may use an existing transactional database or coordination service to allocate monotonically increasing epochs. Each worker includes its epoch on writes to the resource, and the resource rejects epochs lower than the greatest accepted. The check must happen where the side effect occurs. If an object store or external API cannot compare epochs, a separate transactional publish step can determine which result becomes visible. A lease expiry by itself is weaker: an old worker can act after its time has passed unless the resource rejects it.

The election timeout balances detection speed and false elections. Too short, and normal latency or GC pauses cause frequent leader changes. Too long, and failover is slow. Randomized timeouts help avoid multiple candidates repeatedly starting together. Place voters across independent failure domains and check how many must remain reachable. During a partition, a minority should stop authoritative work rather than electing its own competing leader.

## Keep work restartable

Leadership can change while a job is halfway done. Persist progress at safe checkpoints and give each job a stable identity. Make repeated steps idempotent or detect already completed work. The new leader needs to distinguish committed work from attempts by the old leader. If a job holds external resources, define cleanup or expiry for abandoned attempts. A leader should avoid acknowledging a user operation before its durable ownership and progress records are committed.

Monitor election frequency, time without a leader, lease-renewal margin, rejected stale epochs, and jobs recovered after handoff. Test process pauses, delayed packets, and an old leader returning after a new one has completed work. A design that behaves only during clean shutdown has not solved leader election.

## Design review

What resource enforces the current leader's epoch? Can an old leader perform a side effect after takeover? How quickly must failover happen? Can a new leader safely replay or resume unfinished work?
