# Design: ride matching

Ride matching connects a rider request with an available driver while balancing pickup time, marketplace health, and fairness. It is a constrained assignment problem over changing supply and demand. A useful design separates high-rate driver location ingestion from durable trip state, and separates candidate retrieval from reservation and acceptance. Nearby search finds possible drivers; it does not safely assign one.

## Supply, demand, and candidates

Driver apps send location and availability updates with observation time, accuracy, and a session identity. A location service stores the latest position in a hot geospatial index with a short TTL; durable trip and driver state lives in a transactional store. Ignore stale pings and distinguish a driver who is online from one who is eligible for a particular vehicle, region, or ride class. The rider request records pickup, destination, requested service, and an idempotency key so network retries do not create multiple trips.

The matcher searches spatial cells around pickup, expanding radius as needed. A geospatial index quickly produces candidates, then the service removes stale, busy, ineligible, or restricted drivers. Rank by estimated pickup time rather than straight-line distance alone; routing ETA incorporates road networks, direction, traffic, and pickup constraints. Batch candidate scoring to avoid one routing call per driver. The nearest driver may not optimize system-wide wait if accepting that driver starves another request, so a short matching batch can consider multiple requests and drivers together. The product may also weight driver preferences, cancellation risk, accessibility, or marketplace balance.

## Reservation and offer state machine

Before sending an offer, reserve the driver atomically with a lease and fencing version. Two matchers may race for the same driver, and a network timeout can cause duplicate offers. A conditional state transition from `available` to `reserved` must have one winner. The offer includes a stable trip ID, reservation token, expiry, and accept action. On acceptance, confirm the reservation and transition the trip to matched. On rejection or timeout, release it only if the token still matches; an old delayed worker must not clear a newer reservation. Offer retries use the same identity and preserve bounded timeout behavior.

If no one accepts, rematch by expanding the search, trying the next ranked candidates, or changing dispatch policy. Exclude recently rejected drivers for that request for a cooling period. Bound attempts and communicate progress to the rider. A cancellation racing with acceptance needs a conditional transition so the trip cannot become matched after the rider cancels. Durable events or an outbox publishes ride state to notifications and billing.

## Marketplace operation

Partition dispatch by city or region to keep candidate data local, but allow boundary spillover near market edges. Monitor driver-location age, candidate counts, offer acceptance, pickup ETA error, rematch count, reservation conflicts, and unserved demand. Protect the system during a demand surge with admission shaping and a user-visible wait estimate. Test two riders competing for one driver, stale location, late acceptance after expiry, matcher crash, geospatial cell boundary, and route service outage. A safe fallback may use nearest fresh drivers and a simpler score, but must preserve atomic reservation.

## Design review

How are stale drivers excluded? Is ETA or distance the ranking objective? Where is reservation made atomic, and what fences late workers? How do concurrent trips avoid claiming one driver? What bounded policy handles no response or no eligible supply?
